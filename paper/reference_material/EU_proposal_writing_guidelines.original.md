# Writing guidelines for KINTSUGI proposal documents

These rules apply to the KINTSUGI concept note and future proposal text unless a call template imposes a different requirement. They govern both the argument and the Word document. Write in **academic British English** that an industrial partner and an evaluator can understand on a first reading.

## 1. Make each paragraph do one job

- State the project concept early. Give the reader the problem, the proposed process, and the expected result before listing technologies.
- Use one main claim per paragraph. Explain the causal link between a technical method and the problem it solves.
- Build a continuous argument across sentences and paragraphs. If an opening sentence introduces a name, analogy or principle, explain how it leads to the project concept before moving to technical details. Each paragraph should follow from the question or claim established by the previous one.
- Read adjacent sentences together: make the relationship between problem, method, evidence and expected result explicit. Do not use a citation or an attractive opening fact as a detached preface.
- Distinguish a planned contribution from an established fact, a call requirement, a target, and an open design choice. Do not present an ambition as a measured outcome.
- Use specific verbs and objects: *inspect returned components*, *update the digital twin*, *select a recovery route*, *verify function*. Replace vague claims such as *drive transformation* or *unlock potential*.
- Name the technical challenge precisely. In this proposal, heterogeneity of returned products, incomplete condition data, variable disassembly paths, and factory-level integration matter more than general statements about the circular economy.
- State the distinctive research advance once, then show how the methods and pilots test it. Do not restate the same promise in the concept, work plan, outcomes, and impact paragraphs.
- Keep a two-page partner note selective. It should establish the concept and invite partners to shape details; it is not a compressed full proposal.

## 2. Use acronyms selectively

- Use familiar acronyms without expansion when the intended EU research and industrial audience will recognise them immediately. In these documents, **AI, CAD and TRL** may appear from their first occurrence without definition.
- For less familiar acronyms that recur, write the full term followed immediately by its acronym in parentheses at the **first occurrence in the running text**. Use the acronym alone afterwards.
- Do not collect expansions in an opening glossary or acronym list. The reader should meet each definition in the sentence where it becomes relevant.
- Expand an acronym only if it will be used again. If it appears once, write the term in full and omit the acronym.
- Apply the first-use rule to specialised technical and institutional acronyms, such as *original equipment manufacturer (OEM)* if repeated. Check the title, metadata, tables, figures, captions and footnotes for earlier occurrences. If recognition is uncertain, prefer the full term.
- Established project names and official topic identifiers, such as **KINTSUGI** and **HORIZON-CL4-2027-01-MAT-PROD-03**, are identifiers rather than acronyms to invent an expansion for.

## 3. Use restrained academic prose

- Avoid em dashes (`—`). Use a full stop, comma, colon, or parentheses where the relationship is clear.
- Do not use vertical bars (`|`) as separators in titles, subtitles, metadata or running text. Write one coherent phrase or separate items with sentences or line breaks.
- Avoid mirrored constructions such as *not only X but also Y*, *not X but Y*, and *X rather than Y* as a routine way to introduce a claim. State the intended proposition directly.
- Remove redundant pairs and repeated conclusions. Do not say *adaptive*, *integrated*, *innovative*, or *sustainable* in every paragraph; identify the mechanism once and use the term only where it adds meaning.
- Avoid promotional adjectives and unsupported superlatives, including *groundbreaking*, *revolutionary*, *game-changing*, *unique*, and *world-leading* unless independently substantiated.
- Avoid stock transitions and AI-like phrasing such as *delve into*, *foster*, *leverage*, *pivotal*, *robust*, *seamless*, *in today's rapidly evolving landscape*, and *it is worth noting*. Necessary technical terms remain acceptable when used precisely.
- Do not force a three-item list, parallel sentence rhythm, an inflated conclusion, or a generic “challenges and future prospects” paragraph. Use a list only when its structure helps the reader compare or act.
- Keep headings informative and few. Avoid decorative boldface, slogan-like headings, emojis, and tables that merely hold prose.
- Check each sentence for a concrete subject and for words that can be deleted without losing meaning.

The [Wikipedia field guide to signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing) is an **editing prompt**, not a detector or an authority on the proposal. Its useful warnings include promotional tone, vague attribution, superficial analysis, repeated negative parallelism, formulaic lists, and overuse of em dashes. Apply editorial judgement; the goal is accurate, natural prose.

## 4. Support claims and outcomes

- Cite external facts, numerical baselines, and prior art close to the claim they support. Prefer peer-reviewed research, standards bodies, authoritative reports, and direct industrial evidence.
- Treat the funding call as the governing brief for the proposal. Identify the topic in the document metadata, but do not cite the call or work programme as a footnote in the partner concept note.
- Use **real Word footnotes**. Place the automatic superscript number immediately after the relevant sentence or clause. Put the complete, verifiable reference at the bottom of that page. Do not type a superscript manually or collect references as an end list in the two-page note.
- Footnotes are for **external evidence**: academic publications, standards, authoritative reports and datasets, and important source links. Do not cite internal drafts, working notes, prompts, or other files from this project folder as evidence in the proposal.
- Include author or institution, title, year or version, page or section where relevant, and a stable DOI or URL when available. Reuse a source judiciously; do not attach the same citation to every sentence.
- Quantify expected effects only when a baseline, method, and target are specified. Use *will assess* or *aims to* for results that have not been measured.
- Define the evaluation setting, comparison with current practice, and the proposed indicators. For KINTSUGI these may include assessment effort, cycle time, recovery yield, quality, operator workload, cost, and environmental impact.
- Verify that the final text still addresses all five technical areas in the topic, the intended factory and on-site pilots, starting and target TRL, business case, safety, skills, standards, and exploitation in Europe. Do not add a detailed value chain until the consortium selects one.

## 5. Word layout

- Use A4 pages. The body font is **Times New Roman, 11 pt**. Apply the same family and size to footnotes; a slightly larger bold title is acceptable. Avoid blue everywhere, including headings, hyperlinks, rules, and table shading. Use black text and restrained greys only when needed.
- **Justify body paragraphs.** Keep headings left aligned. Use moderate line spacing and paragraph spacing; do not create gaps with repeated empty paragraphs.
- Keep the two-page concept note to two pages. Make room by editing prose before reducing type size or margins.
- Use actual Word styles for title, headings, body, and footnotes. Check that no style or direct formatting restores blue theme colours.
- Use tables only for material that readers must scan across repeated fields. Keep partner roles compact and legible.
- Let Word number footnotes automatically. Inspect the superscript markers and the note text in the rendered document.

## 6. Final editorial and visual check

1. Read the title and first paragraph alone: they must identify the subject, objective, and proposed contribution. Then read paragraph openings in sequence and check that each advances the same argument.
2. Remove repetition and test whether every technical term carries information.
3. Check each factual or numerical claim against its external source and each proposed claim against the current concept. Check specialised acronyms at first use, while leaving familiar ones unexpanded.
4. Search for em dashes, vertical separators, mirrored negative constructions, blue formatting, manually typed footnote numbers, and unsupported superlatives.
5. Render every Word page and inspect body alignment, footnotes, page breaks, tables, and legibility before sharing.
